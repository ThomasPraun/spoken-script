# Writing to be said

Universal rules for a script that will be spoken on camera. Language-specific pronunciation traps, speaking rate and mark labels are in `languages/<code>.md`.

## Sentences

- **One idea per sentence.** If a sentence carries two, split it.
- **Breath test.** Any sentence you can't say comfortably in one breath at speaking pace is too long. Most should be under ~15 words; a few very short ones (1 to 4 words) create punch.
- **Main point first.** Subject and verb early, detail after. The ear holds the start of a sentence badly while waiting for the verb.
- **Concrete over abstract.** A noun you can picture beats a concept. "A 200-euro bill" beats "a significant expense".
- **Active verbs.** Someone does something.
- **Repetition is allowed.** On the page it's redundant; out loud it helps the viewer keep up. Repeat the key term instead of switching to synonyms.

## Punctuation

The speaker reads the script live, usually from a teleprompter or a phone, with no time to parse. Punctuation is how the page tells them how to say it, so it has to be correct and complete.

- **Period**: ends every thought. A new idea starts a new sentence. More periods, shorter sentences, clearer delivery.
- **Comma**: marks a small breath or a break in meaning. Use it wherever the grammar of the language requires it (vocatives, interjections, introductory phrases, lists, inserted clauses) and wherever the speaker should breathe. Never let a long sentence run without one.
- **Question marks**: every real question gets one, so the speaker raises the intonation. In Spanish, the opening `¿` goes exactly where the question starts, even mid-sentence, so the speaker knows before saying it.
- **Exclamation marks**: mark real energy, surprise or a raised voice (`¡` opening too in Spanish). Use them where the speaker would actually lift the voice. One on every line flattens them and reads like an ad.
- **Quotation marks**: mark a change of voice. Use them when the speaker quotes someone or imitates a voice ("y me dice: «no hay lugar»"), when they mention a word as a word, or for irony ("el 'gratis'"). They tell the speaker to shift tone for that stretch. Keep one quote style consistently through the script.
- **Ellipsis**: only for a deliberate trailing-off or suspense. Not as a generic pause.
- **Colon**: fine to introduce a quote or a short list. Avoid it as a reveal device ("The best part: it's free."), where a period works better out loud.
- **Semicolon, dash, parentheses**: avoid. They're written-language tools; out loud they create run-ons or asides the listener can't follow. Replace with a period or a comma.
- **Punctuation comes before marks.** `/` and `//` add pauses that punctuation can't express (a beat before a reveal). They never replace a comma or a period.

## Words

- Prefer the word the speaker would use with a friend over the precise but formal one.
- Avoid words the speaker would stumble on: long words, clusters of similar sounds, words with ambiguous pronunciation.
- One number per sentence. Three figures in a row is a spreadsheet read aloud; the ear keeps none of them. Give the one that matters and let the screen hold the rest.
- If a claim depends on an assumption (a rate, a period, a currency), say the assumption out loud, briefly. On-screen text alone isn't enough: many viewers only listen.
- Write numbers, dates, symbols and abbreviations the way they're said. Round numbers when precision doesn't matter ("almost half" over "47.3%").
- Name the thing again instead of "the former / the latter" or a pronoun that's far from its noun.

## Rhythm and attention

- Vary sentence length on purpose. Long, short. Short.
- Change something every few seconds in short-form (visual, pace, angle, new beat) and at least every 30 to 60 seconds in long-form (re-hook, story, question, example).
- Signpost out loud. The viewer can't see headings: "two things", "the second one", "and here's where it gets weird".
- Open loops early ("I'll show you the mistake at the end") and close every loop you open.
- Speak to one person. "You", not "you all" or "the audience".

## Performance marks

Marks tell the speaker how to say it and the editor what to show. The most useful one is the **intention** of each beat, as a verb (`craft.md` §9): it tells the speaker what they're doing to the viewer, which produces the right tone on its own. Use the labels from the language file. Universal symbols:

| Mark | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| `/` | extra short pause (a beat), beyond what the punctuation already gives |
| `//` | long pause (let it land) |
| `**word**` | stress this word |
| `[LABEL: ...]` | stage direction: visual, on-screen text, pace, tone, sound. The label words come from the language file |

Use marks where they change the delivery, not on every line. A script drowning in marks is as hard to read as one with none.

## Read-aloud check

Before delivering, read the full script at speaking pace, start to finish:

1. Mark every place you'd stumble, run out of breath or lose the thread, and every missing or misplaced comma or period.
2. Fix those, then read again.
3. Time it with the language file's speaking rate and compare with the target.
