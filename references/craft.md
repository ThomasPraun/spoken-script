# Craft: what makes spoken words not flat

`ai-patterns.md` removes what sounds machine-made. This file adds what makes words worth listening to. The goal is eloquence with plain words: simple vocabulary, precise choices, rhythm and life. Distilled from what great playwrights, orators and live storytellers have in common; examples are shapes to learn from, never lines to copy.

The one idea behind all of it: **flat text is general, eloquent text is specific.** A model drifts to the most probable sentence, which is the one anyone could say about anything. Each section below is a way of choosing the less probable, truer one.

## 1. Material before prose

Generic scripts come from writing straight from the topic. Before drafting, gather raw material yourself, from your knowledge and the research step, and write it down as a list (not shown to the user unless asked):

- 2 or 3 concrete cases, scenes or examples where the topic happens to someone;
- real numbers or facts with a source;
- one common mistake or misconception, and why people fall into it;
- one surprising detail most people don't know;
- one opinion the speaker could defend that someone could disagree with;
- objects, places, sounds or gestures tied to the topic;
- one image or comparison taken from the topic's own world.

Then pick the strongest few and build the script on them. Everything must be true or clearly framed as hypothetical ("pongamos que..."); a vivid invented fact is still invented.

## 2. Concrete over abstract

The ear can't hold concepts; it holds pictures. Climb down the ladder of abstraction until you reach something you can see:

> "financial problems" → "debts" → "the credit card bill" → "the bill you leave unopened on the table"

- **Show the thing, not its category.** Not "a significant expense", but "the 300 dollars of the dentist".
- **One sensory detail per scene** is enough: a sound, an object, a light, a smell.
- **Example before concept.** Tell the scene first, then name the idea. The abstraction lands because the listener already lived it.

## 3. The right word, not the difficult one

Eloquence isn't vocabulary. Churchill's advice was that short words are best, and old words, when short, are best of all. Choose the word that is exact, not the one that sounds educated.

- **Strong verbs carry the sentence.** "Se lo devoró" over "lo consumió rápidamente". A precise verb removes the need for an adverb.
- **Fewer adjectives, better nouns.** If the noun is right, the adjective is usually redundant.
- **Everyday words used precisely** beat rare words. The test: would the speaker say this word to a friend? If not, find the one they would, and make it exact.

## 4. Rhythm

Listeners feel rhythm before they understand meaning. Monotone sentence length is the most common reason good content sounds flat.

- **Vary length deliberately.** A run of medium sentences, then a short one. The short one hits because of what came before.
- **End-weight.** The strongest word goes at the end of the sentence, where the voice lands and the listener remembers. "Lo que te cuesta caro es esperar", not "esperar es lo que te cuesta caro".
- **Build, then release.** Two or three rising sentences, then a short drop. That's where a pause (`//`) belongs.
- **Silence is a word.** A pause before the key line is more powerful than an adjective in it.

## 5. Figures, used sparingly

The great speeches are remembered for a handful of devices. Their power comes from scarcity: one or two per script, at the moments that matter, never as decoration.

| Device | What it does | Shape |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Anaphora | Repeats the opening of consecutive sentences to build momentum (King's "I have a dream", Churchill's "We shall fight") | "No es falta de tiempo. No es falta de ganas. Es que nadie te lo explicó." Only when each repetition adds something |
| Antithesis | Places two opposites side by side | "Ahorrás en lo que ves y gastás en lo que no ves." |
| Chiasmus | Reverses the order of a phrase (Kennedy's "Ask not what your country can do for you...") | "No usás el celular; el celular te usa a vos." Once per script at most |
| Rising three | Three items, each longer or stronger than the last. A triad is only earned when the content has three parts *and* they escalate | "Lo probé. Lo medí. Lo dejé de usar a la semana." |
| Callback | Brings back an image or line from the start at the end, transformed | Open with the unopened bill; close with it opened |
| Extended image | One concrete comparison, sustained instead of mixed (King turned civil rights into a bounced check) | Pick one image from the topic's own world and stay with it |

A forced triad is a tell; an earned rising three is rhetoric. The difference is whether the third item is the strongest and whether the content really has three parts.

## 6. Images of your own

Stock images are as generic as stock phrases: "un viaje", "una montaña rusa", "la punta del iceberg", "un arma de doble filo", "game-changer". The listener has heard them a thousand times and stops listening.

- Take the comparison from the topic's own world or the speaker's life. A cook explains money with recipes; a mechanic explains health with engines.
- One image per idea. Don't mix metaphors.
- If an image needs explaining, it's the wrong image.

## 7. Tell it as a scene

Live storytellers agree on a few things:

- **Anecdote, then reflection** (Ira Glass). A sequence of events pulls the listener forward; a moment of reflection tells them why it matters. Alternate them. Events without reflection are trivia; reflection without events is a lecture.
- **Every story is about a change** (Matthew Dicks). Find the moment something shifted, even a small one, and build toward it. Open in the opposite state, so the change has distance to cover.
- **Stakes** (The Moth). What could be gained or lost. A lost suitcase is nothing; a lost suitcase with the only photo of your grandmother is a story. If there's nothing at stake, there's no reason to keep listening.
- **Start late.** Enter the story as close to the change as possible. Cut the setup that the listener doesn't need.
- **Present tense for the key moment.** "Y entonces abro la puerta y..." pulls the listener into the scene. Past tense tells, present tense shows.
- **Direct speech.** "Y me dice: «¿Vos estás seguro?»" beats "me preguntó si estaba seguro". It gives the speaker another voice to play and the scene comes alive. Mark it with quotation marks.

## 8. Contrast and tension

Talks that move people keep alternating between what is and what could be (Nancy Duarte's analysis of great speeches). Flat scripts state; living scripts pull between two poles.

- **Problem, then possibility, then problem again**, each time sharper, before the resolution.
- **"But" and "so" drive tension**; "and then" kills it.
- **Admit the obstacle.** A speaker who names the real difficulty sounds more credible than one who sells.

## 9. Intention: the actor's tool

Actors avoid flat delivery by giving every line a playable verb: what the character wants from the other person (to convince, to warn, to confess, to provoke, to comfort). Lines without intention come out recited.

- Give each beat of the script one intention, written as a verb: *sorprender, advertir, confesar, desafiar, tranquilizar, burlarse*. Change it when the beat changes.
- Write the line so the intention is in the words, not only in the mark. A warning sounds like a warning even on paper.
- Mark it with the intention label from the language file so the speaker knows what they're doing, not only how they sound. "Intention: warn" helps more than "tone: serious".
- **Subtext**: sometimes the strongest line says less than it means. Understatement trusts the listener.

## 10. Someone is talking

A speech is credible because a person is behind it (Aristotle's ethos), moving because it touches what the listener feels (pathos), and convincing because it makes sense (logos). Generic scripts have only logos.

- **An opinion.** The speaker takes a position. "I think this is overrated, and here's why" beats a balanced survey.
- **A cost or vulnerability.** What the speaker got wrong, what it cost them. Only real ones, from the speaker or the brief.
- **Humor where the voice has it.** Usually from a precise observation, not a joke inserted.
- **Speak to one person** and assume they're smart.

## Tests

Run these on the draft, line by line where they apply:

1. **Swap test**: could any creator say this line about any other topic? If yes, it's generic. Rewrite it with something only this topic, or this speaker, can say.
2. **Picture test**: can the listener see something in each section? If a section has no concrete image, case or detail, add one.
3. **Stake test**: does the viewer know what they gain or lose by the first quarter of the script?
4. **Change test**: does something change between the start and the end (a belief, a situation, a feeling)?
5. **Ear test**: read it aloud. Where does the rhythm go flat? Where does the strongest word fall in the middle of a sentence instead of at the end?
6. **Intention test**: can you name the verb of each beat?
7. **Scarcity test**: count the figures. More than two or three in a short script means decoration.
